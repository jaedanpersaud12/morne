# Chapter 1 — Dust

Kit's father fell asleep three times before lunch, and the first time, she laughed.

He was in the driver's seat of the taxi in the yard, with both front doors open for the breeze, his chin on his chest and his mouth open, snoring over the little book where he wrote his fares. When she knocked on the roof, he jumped so hard the pen went down between the seats. He felt for it with two fingers and gave up.

"Dust," he said, and pointed up.

The sky was the colour of weak tea. Sahara dust had blown in overnight, four thousand miles of it, and turned the sun into a dull orange coin you could look straight at. It came a few times a year and got in your eyes and your throat. Everyone on the island blamed it for everything: headaches, bad moods, dogs that wouldn't eat.

"It's November," Kit said. "Dust comes in June."

"Dust don't read calendar."

Ambrose Wick had driven a taxi for sixteen years, in a shirt pressed every morning, even on a Sunday, with the crease in his trousers ironed sharp, and he washed the car before the sun got hot whether or not anybody was going anywhere. On Sundays he sat in it with the church service on the car radio, because the car was the only thing he owned outright, and a man should sit in what he owns. In sixteen years she had never seen him sleep in it in the day. She let that go, because it was Sunday and the dust was in everyone's head, and went back to the washing.

Quarry Trace was nine houses on a road that went up the hill and stopped. Concrete houses, galvanize roofs, burglar-proofing on every window, black water tanks up on stands in every yard, and two of them still had an upstairs half-built, with rebar sticking out of the roof waiting for the money. At the top the road gave up at a rusted chain-link gate, and behind it was the quarry, a bite out of the hill that the trucks had stopped coming for six years ago. From up there you could see the whole south of the island: Morne Bay curved around its harbour, the ferry dock, the two church steeples, and the sea going out flat and blue to nothing.

From the bottom you saw the bend.

The flat was two concrete rooms under somebody else's empty upstairs, the first house on the road, a bedroom, a front room with a hotplate, and a bathroom with a window that didn't close. It was the two of them in it, her and Ambrose, and had been for eight months, since her mother stayed in town and the rent in town got to be too much for one.

The second time was around ten o'clock. She was pegging a sheet when she looked over and his head was down again. This time nothing in her wanted to laugh, so she went over to the car and shook his shoulder.

He woke up slow, and when his eyes opened they took a second to find her.

"Daddy. Go inside and lie down."

"In the day?" He rubbed his whole face with both hands. "I not sick. Is the dust."

"You said that."

"Because is true."

She didn't go back to the washing. She sat on the front step, where she could see him through the open door of the car, and she didn't take her eyes off him.

---

The others came down the hill the way they always did, as a noise first.

A lot of voices at once, nobody waiting for anybody to finish, and then the Pells' hibiscus and there they were, coming round to the bend: six of them, and the little one, Olli, who was seven and counted himself.

"No," Juno Vale said. "Tock. No."

"The goat," her brother said.

All of them went. Not laughed, went. Somebody bent in half. Somebody had to put a cooler down. Juno hit her brother in the arm and laughed harder than any of them, head back.

Kit stood on the step with a clothes peg in her hand.

She had lived under these people for eight months. They had grown up together in the houses up the road, every one of them, and she had come in March with two suitcases and a taxi. They had tried. Lyra had come down the first week with a plate of pelau and stayed an hour, talking, mostly to herself. Lonny waved from the pickup every morning with both hands off the wheel. Sol had lent her a phone charger in April and never asked for it back. Twice somebody had started to explain the goat, and both times the rest of them had shouted over the details until it came apart. It was ten years of them, and you couldn't hand somebody ten years. Every joke had a footnote, and the footnote was a childhood she hadn't been in.

Juno noticed. Juno always noticed. Lyra waved, big, with her whole arm, at the upstairs. Nobody lived in the upstairs.

"Morning, Kit."

She was tall, with her hair up in a puff and wire glasses she pushed up with one finger. She had been top of the whole island last year, with a picture in the paper that Kit had seen in a barbershop in town before she ever knew Juno's face. Nobody on the hill had been surprised. She was going to be a doctor. That was settled.

"Morning."

"We going down the beach. You want to come?"

She asked every time and meant it every time, and every time Kit said the same thing.

"I have to help my father."

"Okay." No hurt in it. "Mr. Wick! Good morning!"

"Morning, Miss Vale." Her father leaned out of the car as Lonny brought the Hales' pickup down the hill behind them. His voice was his own voice, his hand up and steady, and something in Kit's chest that had been clenched since ten o'clock let go a little.

Fen was last into the tray of the pickup. He always was.

He stopped by the front step on the way but didn't look at her, exactly. He looked at the ground beside her left foot, which was his version, and pulled one earphone out.

"It's not even a goat," he said. "I'll tell you later."

"You said that last time."

"I know."

The pickup went round the bend and down toward the junction, six in the tray and Lonny driving too fast, and her father called "Mind the police!" after it, like every time. But he called it late, when the pickup was already gone.

---

The island was heavy all day.

The church service on the car radio kept stopping. The pastor would lose his place in a verse and go quiet before starting the verse again. Mrs. Moss at number four was asleep in her verandah chair at half past nine with her Bible open on her chest, and Mrs. Moss was a woman who caught other people doing that. The yellow dog at the Pells' didn't bark at the gas truck, which came late, and the driver sat in his cab for a long minute after he parked, hands on the wheel, before he shook himself and got down.

"Dust," her father said when she told him. "Everybody feeling it."

He was feeling it. When she brought him the thermos he drank all of it, and she watched his eyelids anyway. Twice she had to say his name. The second time he laughed at himself, a short embarrassed sound, then got out of the car and did ten squats in the yard in his pressed shirt. It was so unlike him that she laughed too, and for a minute it was almost fine.

The pickup came back at two. That was early.

Nobody in it was talking, and the cooler was still heavy. Juno jumped down out of the tray at the bend and came straight to the car, not to Kit, to the car, and looked in at Kit's father for a second too long before she turned.

"Lifeguard was asleep on his tower," she said. "We thought he was dead. Lonny climbed up. He wasn't dead, he was asleep, and we couldn't wake him. And the doubles lady by the car park, her pot was burning with her sitting right next to it. So we came back." Fast and steady, the voice she used when she was in charge of something. Then, quieter: "Your father okay?"

"He keeps falling asleep."

Juno looked at him again through the windscreen. Her mouth did something small.

"Keep him awake," she said. "I don't know why. Just do it."

They went up the hill, quieter than they'd come down.

Kit kept him awake. She talked to him and made him stand, made him walk up to the Pells' gate and back, which he did, grumbling. Then he sat down heavily and told her she was worse than her mother, said sorry, and then said it again.

At a quarter to five, back in the driver's seat with her standing right there at the door, he put the pen down in the middle of a word.

"Daddy."

His chin went to his chest. His breath went in and out. Slow. Deep. Even.

"Daddy. Daddy, wake up."

She shook him, slapped his face, not hard and then hard, and when that did nothing she poured what was left in the thermos over his head, lukewarm coffee running down his neck into his pressed collar. He didn't move. She pinched the skin on the back of his hand, the clinic nurse's trick, and it stood up in a little tent and went down slow.

His skin was cool.

That was the thing that turned her stomach over. It was thirty-four degrees in that car with both doors open, and her father, who sweated through his shirt by noon every day of his life, was cool to the touch.

She took out her phone and dialled 999. It rang a long time before it stopped ringing and nothing happened, no voice, no click, an open line with nobody on it.

She hung up. Her hands were shaking now. She dialled the other number, the one she knew by heart and had never once used.

It rang and rang, somewhere down in Morne Bay, and nobody picked up. She didn't let herself think about whether that was the dust or just her mother.

She stood at the car door with the phone in her hand. For a few seconds there was nothing in her head at all, only the radio and the sound of him breathing, and she let it be nothing, because the alternative was screaming and screaming wouldn't move a man off a chair.

Then she thought it through, like he would have.

On his side, so he wouldn't choke. Something under his head. Then help.

He weighed more than two of her, but she got her arms hooked under his and pulled until he slid out of the seat and down onto the concrete of the yard, in the car's shade. She rolled him onto his side and pushed the folded towel he kept on the seat under his head, and through all of it he breathed, in and out, like nothing in the world was happening to him.

Then she ran.

Up the hill in her slippers until the left one went, and then barefoot on the hot road, past the hibiscus, to Juno.

The Vales' front door was open. It was never open.

Granny Vale was on the kitchen floor with her white Sunday headtie still perfect. She hadn't fallen. She was flat on her back with her hands at her sides, the way you'd lie down on purpose. Olli was wedged between the fridge and the wall with his knees up.

Juno was kneeling beside her grandmother with two fingers on the old woman's wrist, lips moving, counting. She looked up, and her face was calm. Her eyes were not.

"Your father?"

Kit nodded.

"Breathing? Slow?"

"Twelve." She hadn't known she'd counted. "Twelve in a minute."

Juno stared at her. Then down at her grandmother. Then back.

"Her too," she said, and stood up fast. "Is he on his side?"

"Yes."

"Something under his head?"

"Yes."

"Good. Good, Kit." She already had her phone out, already had Olli by the arm. "Go back down and stay with him. Nobody comes up past the bend, in or out, till I say. I'm sending Tock to every house. Go."

Kit went.

Halfway down the hill she slowed, because her legs had gone strange, and because of what Juno's face had done when she said *her too*. Kit knew what fear looked like on Juno; she'd seen it in August, the day Olli fell off the Hales' tank stand. This was flatter.

Two houses. Two grown people. Same breathing.

She started running again, and the whole way down she was making a list of what else she should have done, water, a wet cloth, the fan turned to face him, until she came past the Pells' hibiscus to the top of the bend and stopped dead.

The corbeaux had come down.

All day they'd sat on the light pole at the bend, four or five of them, the big black vultures with their wings half open, same as always. Now there were a dozen. More. They were on the galvanize of the flat. They were on the roof of the taxi. They were on the yard fence on either side of it, hunched and patient, with their bald red heads turned, every one of them, toward the open door of the car.

Toward her father, on the ground beside it.

Kit stood in the road with the dust in her throat. The birds thought he was dead. And the birds were never wrong about that.



# Chapter 2 — Count

Juno gave it thirty seconds.

She had sent Kit back down the hill and Tock out with his phone to every house on the road. She had put Olli on the couch with the TV on and told him Granny was sleeping hard, in the most bored voice she could find, and he had believed her, because he always did. Then she had walked into the downstairs bathroom, shut the door, sat on the edge of the tub, and shaken so hard her teeth clicked.

She let it happen. She counted it. Thirty seconds was what it got, because two years ago, in the worst stretch of a bad year, she had learned that if you let the shaking have as long as it wanted, it took everything.

At thirty she stood up, ran the tap, put cold water on her face, and looked in the mirror until she recognised herself.

Then she went back to the kitchen floor and did it properly.

Two years ago, hiding in the school library from a lunch period she couldn't face, she had found a nursing textbook nobody had checked out since 1998 and read it cover to cover, because reading still worked when nothing else did. The chapter on coma had a table: breathing, pulse, pupils, response to pain. She had learned the table the way she learned every table, without deciding to.

Granny was breathing twelve times a minute. Pulse fifty-two. Pupils small, and they didn't care about the kitchen light. When Juno pressed a knuckle hard into the bone in the middle of her grandmother's chest, where the book said, Granny didn't flinch.

Deep coma. That was what the table called it.

Dot Vale was seventy-four years old and five feet tall, and in Juno's whole life she had never gone an hour without an opinion. She had raised three grandchildren in this house after their mother died and their father went to Canada. She wore a headtie every day, a different colour for each day of the week, white on Sundays. In August, when the scholarship letter came, she had read it twice at this table with her glasses on and said, "So. That's done. Now you have to keep it."

That was Granny. On the floor now, with the white headtie still perfect and nothing to say.

Juno picked up the house phone and dialled 999. It rang for a long time before it stopped ringing and did nothing else, no voice, no click, an open line with nobody on it. She listened to the nothing for ten seconds, then put it down.

She tried the hospital in Morne Bay. Same.

She tried her father in Toronto, which was stupid, because what was he going to do from Toronto, and when it went to voicemail she hung up before the beep.

---

They came in pieces over the next half hour, and every one of them brought the same news.

Lonny first. His father was down in the La-Z-Boy. His mother had gone down on the bathroom floor, and he had carried her to the bed before he thought about whether he should. His little brother Bix was eleven and awake and had refused to leave, so Lonny had locked the front door with Bix inside and come anyway. He said it like a confession.

Sol came holding her phone in both hands. Both her parents, on the couch in front of the cricket, with the volume still up. She had turned the TV off, and then turned it back on, because the quiet was worse.

Tock came last, and he came angry, which was how Tock came to everything that scared him. He had stopped at every house on the road: the Pells, the Mosses, the Nettles, where the little girl was crying too hard to talk, and old Mr. Hale at the top of the road, who was ninety and lived alone and had been face down in a puddle of soup.

"All of them," Tock said. "Every grown person. Every one I checked."

"The kids?"

"Kids are fine. Nettle girl's crying, I told her lock the door, I'll come back." He kicked the leg of the table. Not hard. Then harder. "Why are the kids fine?"

Nobody answered that.

Tock put a steel thermos on the counter. "Kit," he said. "From the bend. She made it for her father." He didn't finish that. "She said you'd be up."

It was coffee, too sweet, the way a taxi man would take it. Juno didn't drink coffee. Near three in the morning she drank all of it standing at the counter.

Fen and Lyra didn't come. Lyra called, and Juno put her on speaker on the table so she wouldn't have to say it twice.

"We're not leaving her." Lyra's voice was flat, and in sixteen years Juno had never once heard Lyra be flat about anything. "The night nurse is here. She's asleep too, in the chair. Daddy's on the floor in the hall. Mummy's the same, she looks the same as always. Fen's checking the oxygen. We're staying."

"Okay."

"Juno. What is this."

"I don't know."

A pause. Then Fen, from the other side of the room: "Tank's fine. She's breathing eleven."

"Twelve here," Juno said. "Everybody's twelve, or near it. Kit's father, Granny, all of them."

"Then it's one thing," Fen said, and hung up, because Fen didn't do goodbyes.

The radio on the counter had been playing the same song for twenty minutes. Some old Machel tune from before they were born, start to finish, two seconds of dead air, then the same song. Somebody at the station had put it on and put their head down.

She turned it off.

"Okay," she said. "Listen. Phones work. Power works. Water works. So it's not the island, it's people. Grown people. And it's not just us, because the lifeguard, the doubles lady, that was town. And 999 doesn't answer, and the hospital doesn't answer."

"So it's everybody," Lonny said.

"Maybe. Probably."

"So who do we call?"

"Nobody. There's nobody. It's us."

"For now," Tock said. "They'll wake up. People wake up."

"Coast guard will come," Sol said, to the table. "Or the government. Somebody. By morning somebody's going to come."

Juno let them have it. Half of her believed it too, and the half that didn't wasn't going to help anyone tonight.

"Probably," she said. "But we don't do nothing till then."

Sol put her phone face down on the table, very carefully. Lonny sat on the floor with his back against the cupboards, and Tock didn't move at all.

Juno found a school exercise book in the drawer by the stove, half of it used for Olli's spelling, and tore those pages out. She wrote at the top of a clean one and said it out loud as she wrote, because saying it kept the shaking out of her hands.

"One. The sleepers. They can't drink. Three days without water, that's the number, less in this heat, less if they're old. So that's first. Two—"

She stopped. Her stomach dropped before the thought finished.

"Tock. The Pells. Did you check the crib?"

Tock looked at her.

The Pells had a baby. Eight months old. Juno had held her in September at the christening lime in the Finches' yard, and the baby had grabbed her glasses.

"I checked them," Tock said. "They were in the bed, both of them, I rolled them, I—"

"The crib, Tock."

He was already moving. The door hit the wall behind him.

Sol was crying without making a sound, and Lonny had his head between his knees. Juno stood at the counter with the pen and made herself keep writing.

*Two. Babies. Small kids. Who's alone.*

*Three. Mr. Hale, ninety. Who else old.*

*Four. Town.*

She looked at the word. Morne Bay had fifteen thousand people, maybe. Say a third of them kids. Most of the little ones would be fine, because that was how the island worked: a twelve-year-old with a baby sister would feed the baby, a cousin would take a cousin, a neighbour would hear crying through a wall. But there were houses where the oldest child was four. There were flats where nobody knew the people next door. There was no list of those houses, and no way to make one.

Tock's feet on the road, coming back. Faster.

He came through the door with the baby against his chest. She was screaming, a thin furious sound, red-faced and soaked through her sleepsuit. Tock kept saying "it's okay, it's okay" in a voice that wasn't. Juno had never in her life been so glad to hear anything scream.

"Alive," Tock said. "She was just in there. In the dark. I walked right past the door." His hands were shaking, but he wouldn't give her over. "I walked right past."

"You went back." Juno's voice came out steadier than she was. "Sol, warm water, there's bottles in their kitchen. Lonny, go with Tock to every house again, every room this time. Every crib, every cot, every bathroom. Anybody under five comes here."

"And then?" Lonny said. "Tock, you walk past a baby. A whole baby." Tock looked at him. Then Sol laughed, wet, and Tock said "shut up," and it was the first thing anybody had said all night in a normal voice.

She looked at the list. At the word.

"Then at first light, two of us go down past the bend. Not town. The road. The twelve houses between here and the junction, the ones we know. Miss Orla. The Lark family. Lonny, your girlfriend's street." His head came up. "We knock, we check, we come back. That's what we can do."

"And the rest of town?"

"Town has to do town." She said it flat. "There's a hundred kids like us down there right now doing exactly this. Walking into houses. We're not special. We not special. We just the ones up here."

Nobody argued, though she wished someone would. She wrote *road at first light* under the list, and under that, smaller, *town?*, and left the question mark.

---

She went to number four by herself, because somebody had to and everybody else had a job.

Dr. Moss kept things at home. Granny had sent her over for antibiotics once at nine at night, and he had unlocked a cupboard in his study and handed her a box without writing anything down. The spare key was under the frog on the step, where everybody on the road kept their spare. Dr. Moss was on the hall floor where Tock had put him, on his side, with his reading glasses still on their chain around his neck. Juno stepped over him and said "Sorry, doctor," out loud.

The cupboard key was in his desk, second drawer. People weren't creative about keys.

Inside: a doctor's bag, a box of gloves with the lid torn off, bandages, amoxicillin in four strengths, two of them past date, paracetamol by the hundred, a blood pressure cuff with the velcro gone furry, syringes still in their plastic. And on the bottom shelf, a carton of IV fluid, clear bags, a litre each, the kind they had hung next to Granny when she had her gallbladder out.

She counted them. She counted again.

Six.

Sixteen sleepers. Two litres a day each, at least, in this heat. She did the division standing in Dr. Moss's study with his sleeping body in the hall, and the answer was so small it was almost funny. Less than a day. For six of them. For the rest, nothing.

She took it all anyway.

From the balcony at the top of the house, with the carton at her feet, she could see the whole town. Every light in Morne Bay was on: the coast road, the gas station sign, the two churches lit up white, the ferry lit up in its dock. From up here it looked like any Sunday night.

Nothing moved. No cars. No music from the bars by the harbour, where there was always music. Just dogs, hundreds of them, all across the island, barking at the quiet.

Her phone buzzed. Tock.

*9 houses. 16 adults. 13 kids counting us. 1 baby. all adults down. all kids ok. everybody coming to yours*

She read it twice before she put the phone down on the railing and held onto the railing with both hands, because for a second the balcony had tilted.

Sixteen people who couldn't drink. Six bags. Three days. Granny was seventy-four and Mr. Hale was ninety.

She could lie about the bags. Tell them there were enough and ration in secret, and nobody would know until somebody's mother didn't wake up. Or she could put the carton on the kitchen table and tell twelve kids the truth and watch what it did to them.

She stood there until she'd chosen, then picked up the carton and went down.

The kitchen was full now, kids on every chair and along the walls, the Nettle girl asleep against Lonny with her thumb in her mouth. Bix Hale was eating Granny's bread from the bag, standing at the counter, not asking, and he looked at Juno while he did it. The baby had stopped screaming and was asleep on Tock's shoulder, and Tock hadn't moved in twenty minutes in case she woke.

Juno put the carton on the table where everyone could see it, then opened the notebook and wrote *Day 0* at the top of the list. She had almost written *Day 1* and crossed it out, because she wanted to count from the beginning. She had a feeling she'd be counting a long time.

Her phone rang, a real call this time. Kit, from the bottom of the road.

"There's people at the bend."

Juno stood up, and the kitchen went quiet around her.

"Who?"

"Kids. From the road. Four of them, I think. There's a girl with a baby." Kit's voice dropped. "She's asking for the doctor. She said the Vale girl, up the Trace, she's going to be a doctor. Somebody told her."

Juno looked at the carton. Six bags. She looked at Dr. Moss's leather bag beside it, which she had carried up the hill and not yet opened, because opening it meant finding out what she didn't know how to use.

Sixteen sleepers on one road. Thirteen kids. One baby. One list.

And now four more at the bottom of the road, in the dark, with a baby, asking for a doctor.

"Tell them I'm coming," she said.

She picked up the bag. She was not a doctor. She went down the hill anyway.



# Chapter 3 — Signal

Fen Quill knew the sound of every machine in his mother's room.

The oxygen concentrator was the low one, a hum with a click in it every four seconds. The mattress pump was higher and came on every twenty minutes. The monitor didn't make a sound unless something was wrong, and in eight months it had made a sound three times. He could tell from his bed upstairs which machine was running. He had not meant to learn any of this. It had happened the way you learn the stairs in your own house, without trying, in the dark.

Tonight the hum was normal, the click was normal, and nothing in the room was wrong except everything.

His mother lay in the hospital bed where the dining table used to be, on her back, with the cannula in her nose and her hands on the blanket. Eleven breaths a minute. He had counted four times. Miss Tilda, the night nurse, was asleep in the armchair by the window with her shoes off and her mouth open. His father was on the hall floor, on his side, where Fen had put him two hours ago.

Lyra was on the other side of the bed with her phone in her lap and her thumb moving without stopping.

"Nobody's answering," she said. "Nobody. I messaged everybody."

"Everybody we know is sixteen."

"I messaged Auntie Prue. I messaged the church group. I messaged Mummy's oncologist, Fen, I have his number from the thing in July."

"They're all asleep."

"You don't know that."

He didn't answer. He looked at the concentrator's little green light.

Lyra put the phone down and reached over to straighten the blanket where it didn't need straightening. Their mother's face was turned a little toward her, the way it had been for weeks, because the pillow was shaped that way. Her skin had the yellow look it had had since August. None of it was new.

"She looks the same," Lyra said.

"Yeah."

"Everybody else's parents look like they got knocked out. Lonny's dad was in the La-Z-Boy with his mouth open. Mummy just looks like Mummy."

"Yeah."

"Fen."

He looked up.

"Is it bad," Lyra said, "that she looks better?"

Something in his chest went tight and then loose. He wanted to give her a real answer. She was the only person in the world he could give one to.

"She's not in pain," he said. "Look at her hands."

Lyra looked. Their mother's hands were open on the blanket. For months they had been curled, the fingers closed on nothing, even in sleep, even with the morphine. Now they were open.

"Oh," Lyra said.

"So no. I don't think it's bad."

She didn't cry. She put her hand over their mother's open one and left it there. After a while she said, "If they all wake up tomorrow, she wakes up into that again."

"I know."

"I don't want her to wake up into that."

"I know," Fen said. "Me neither."

It was the first time either of them had said it out loud. It didn't feel like a terrible thing to say. It felt like putting something heavy down.

---

At ten he went up to the roof.

The Quill house had a flat roof with a water tank and a satellite dish, and a plastic chair Fen had carried up there two years ago and never brought down. He brought the laptop and the dongle, a black thing the size of a lighter that his father had called a waste of money, and that Fen had bought with birthday money anyway, because with it and a length of wire you could hear anything in the air. Taxis. Aircraft. Fishing boats talking at four in the morning. He had spent a lot of nights up here listening to people who didn't know they were being listened to. There was a taxi driver, Pinto, who got on the base channel every night and talked about the price of parts and who was sleeping with who, and Fen had hated him for two years without ever seeing his face. He went looking for Pinto first. Nothing.

The dust had thinned enough to show stars. Below the hill the town had all its lights on and none of its noise.

He started on FM, where the station was still playing the one song. He went through the whole band, and it was the same soca or nothing, the one from last Carnival about the woman with the red bucket, everywhere in February and hated by March. The station's overnight computer had been playing it since Sunday to nobody.

Marine channels. Sixteen, the one every boat kept open for emergencies, was hiss, and he sat on it for five minutes with the volume up.

Then he went looking, and it took twenty minutes.

It was on a channel the fishing boats used for weather, so faint he had to put his ear to the laptop speaker. A boy, older than him from the sound of it, and angry, saying the same thing over and over.

"—anybody. This is the *Lady Marisol* at the fish dock. Anybody with a radio. All the big people sleeping. If you can hear me press the button and talk. Anybody."

Fen looked at the dongle. It couldn't transmit. It could only hear.

He was down the ladder before he'd finished the thought, through the house, past his father in the hall, into the garage. His father's boat radio was on the shelf above the workbench, the handheld they used on Sundays when they took the little boat out, before. It hadn't been charged in a year. He found the charger, plugged it in, and stood in the dark garage watching the light go red, then nothing, then red, for four minutes until the screen came on.

Back on the roof, on the weather channel, the boy was still going.

"—press the button and talk. Anybody."

Fen pressed the button.

"I hear you." It came out too quiet and he said it again. "I hear you. I'm up Quarry Trace. The road to the old quarry, on the south hill."

Silence, long enough that he thought the handheld didn't have the range.

Urine, mostly, and sweat, and under both of those something sweeter that Juno knew from the ward where Granny had her gallbladder out and had hoped never to smell again. Between turns, the spoon. A person to every two sleepers, round the clock, a teaspoon at a time into the cheek. It took an hour to get a cup into somebody. They needed eight cups a day in this heat, and they weren't getting four. There weren't enough hands.

Olli wanted a job. She gave him eyes.

"Their eyes don't shut all the way," she said. "See? If they dry out, they get damaged. So you wet the cloth, you wipe, gentle, then the tape, like this. Just over the lid."

He did it like it was surgery. He went down a row with his tongue between his teeth, wiping and taping, and when he got to Granny he stopped and looked at her for a while.

"Is Granny going to die?"

"No," Juno said, without looking up. "She's too rude to die. God doesn't want her."

Olli laughed, a real one, surprised out of him. Ida looked up from across the room, and Juno felt bad for about a second and then didn't.

Granny's bag had run dry on the second day. They all had. Now the six were on syringes, a plastic one with the needle off, a few drops at a time into the cheek, because a syringe was slower than a spoon and slow was the only way they didn't cough. It was the slowest water in the house.

Mr. Hale was getting the most. Juno had seen to that. Four days, every hour she wasn't turning or sleeping, she had sat with him with the teaspoon. She had given him water she should have spent sleeping. She had given him water she should have given Granny.

On the fourth evening she pinched the skin on the back of his hand. It stayed up.

A ridge, standing on its own, slowly sinking. The book had a word for that too. His lips were cracked, though she put Vaseline on them every hour. His eyes had sunk back in his face so far that Olli's tape didn't touch the lids any more. She counted his breathing at eleven and wrote it down. His pulse, when she counted it, was faster than it had been, and she wrote that down too and didn't like it.

"He's getting more than any of them," Tock said, behind her. He had the baby on his hip. Vee went everywhere with Tock now; nobody had decided that either. "You're giving him more than anybody."

"I know."

"So why's he worse?"

"Because he's ninety." She capped the Vaseline. "Because a cup every two hours isn't what a body needs. It's what we've got."

Tock looked at the rows. "And the rest?"

She didn't answer, and he didn't ask again. He went to turn row three, because the clock had gone off.

At two in the morning, with the torch propped on a book, she was spooning Mr. Hale when his breathing changed.

It went quick and shallow. Then deeper, deeper, his thin chest rising high under the sheet, then shallower again, tapering off. Then nothing.

She counted. Five seconds. Ten. Fifteen.

At twenty-one he breathed again. Quick and shallow, then deeper, building.

The table in the nursing book had a name for this pattern. It had a line underneath the name, in small type, that she had read at fifteen and never needed to know. She knew it now, with the spoon in her hand and water on her fingers. She sat beside him and counted the gaps, and they got longer.

---

He died at twelve minutes past five, with the windows going grey.

The gap came and didn't end. She counted to sixty. She put two fingers on the side of his neck where the book said, and waited, and moved them, and waited again. Then she looked at the kitchen clock through the doorway and wrote the time in the exercise book, because in hospitals somebody always wrote down the time.

*Mr. Hale. 5:12 a.m. Day 5.*

Ida came and knelt on the other side of him and folded her hands. She said a prayer Juno knew. Granny said it every night in the room next to Juno's, through the wall, in exactly that rhythm. Juno knew every word. She couldn't make her mouth do any of them.

It was Tock who said it, out on the porch, with Vee asleep on his shoulder and the sun coming up over the sea. The corbeaux were already on the roof of number nine, hunched against the light. He didn't look at them.

"We can't keep him in there."

"I know."

"It's going to be thirty-one degrees by ten."

"I know, Tock."

"The cemetery's in town."

"I know."

Lonny was sitting on the porch step, where he'd been asleep, or close to it. He stood up.

"I'll dig him."

Juno looked at him.

"Same name as me," Lonny said. "We're not family. Never were. Everybody on the road used to make the joke." He shrugged, and it came out wrong, too big. "Somebody with the name should do it."

They chose the slope behind number nine, Mr. Hale's own house, under the lime tree at the bottom of his garden where the land fell away toward the sea. Lonny got a pickaxe and a spade from the Finches' garage. The first foot was easy, dark topsoil and roots. Under it the island was clay and stone, packed hard. He swung the pickaxe for three hours with his shirt off and his hands wrapped in a dish towel. Nobody saw his hands until after, under the tap, when the water ran off them pink for a while and then clear. The little kids brought him water in relays, then stood at the edge of the hole and watched, because nobody had told them not to.

Halfway down the soil changed. It went darker and cooler, and every spadeful came up threaded with something white and fine, like hair. Lonny knocked it off the blade against his boot without a word and kept digging.

At four feet he hit rock and couldn't go further.

"It's enough," Juno said.

"It's supposed to be six."

"It's enough, Lonny."

They wrapped Mr. Hale in the sheet from his own bed. Olli found an electricity bill on the counter at number nine and came running down the garden holding it up. *Mr. Barnaby Hale.* Ninety years on the road, and nobody at the hole had known it until a seven-year-old sounded it out off a bill.

Lonny climbed down into the grave. When Tock and Juno lowered the sheet to him, he took it and laid the old man on the cool white-threaded floor of the hole as gently as he'd carried every one of them up the drive.

Ida said the prayer again. This time a few of the little ones said it with her.

They stood. Then Tock cleared his throat.

"He used to shout at us," he said. "When the cricket ball went in his yard."

"Every time," Lonny said.

"He gave it back every time, though."

"He kept the good one," Juno said. "The red one. Remember? Form One. He kept it for a year." "You cried," Tock said.

"I didn't cry."

Tock laughed, a short, shocked sound that set off Lonny and then a couple of the little kids who didn't know why it was funny. Then they stopped, and Lonny climbed out and picked up the spade.

Juno stayed after.

The others went back to the house, because the clock would go off in forty minutes and row two needed turning. After filling the hole and stamping the earth down, Lonny had gone to sit under the tap of the Hales' water tank with his clothes on, letting it run over his head. Juno sat under the lime tree with the exercise book on her knees.

Kit was at the fence at the bottom of the garden. She must have come up while they were digging and stood there the whole time with her arms folded, close enough to hear the prayer and far enough to leave if anybody looked at her.

"He gave me fifty cents once," she said. "For the parlour. My first week. He said the new girl should have a sweetie."

"He never gave us anything," Juno said.

"You lot kicked balls in his yard."

Juno laughed before she could stop it. Kit looked pleased with herself for a second, then looked back down the hill toward the bend, where her father was alone.

"I can't stay." She unfolded her arms. "When this is over, I'm coming to the beach."

She was gone down the road before Juno could answer, and Juno sat with that longer than she meant to.

She had given him the most water of anyone. Four days of her own hands, every hour she had. It hadn't been enough.

Granny had had one bag, four days ago. Since then she'd had a syringe, a few drops at a time, the slowest water in the house. She had had less than Mr. Hale. All six of them had.

She opened the book to the grid and did what she always did. She made the numbers do what they did. Cups per day. Days. Age. The skin on the back of a hand that stayed up when you pinched it. The table from the nursing book, which she knew the way she knew her own phone number.

Then she turned to a clean page and wrote the next names, in the order the numbers gave them to her.

The first one was Granny's.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked down the slope, past the bend and the road and the junction, to where the coast road curved into Morne Bay. The hospital sat white on the hill above the harbour.

There was a store room in that hospital with more bags in it than Dr. Moss had ever kept in a cupboard. Every nurse on the island knew where it was. Every nurse on the island was asleep.

The hospital was eleven kilometres away, at the bottom of the hill, in a town where people already knew her name.



# Chapter 6 — Sixteen

Fen Quill had talked to Judge every night for five nights, and every night Judge called at nine.

Not around nine. Nine. Fen had started to believe there was a clock on the deck of the *Lady Marisol* that Judge watched the way Fen watched the generator's fuel gauge, because at the top of the hour the handheld on the roof beside him would click, and hiss, and the voice would come up out of it slow, like somebody walking in from far away.

"Trace. You there?"

On the fifth night Fen was there, because he was always there. He had been on the roof since the funeral. He had watched them carry Mr. Hale down the garden in his own sheet and watched Lonny climb into the hole. Then he had come up the ladder with the laptop and stayed, because up here there was nobody to look at him and the machines made sense.

The generator ran from seven at night to seven in the morning now. Twelve hours, eighteen litres, enough to keep his mother's oxygen going through the dark and the fridge cold for Juno's antibiotics. In the day he ran the concentrator off a car battery and the inverter from his father's boat, which gave him four hours. For the rest of the day his mother breathed room air, and nothing happened. Her breathing stayed at eleven, and the oximeter on her finger stayed at ninety-four. He had noticed that on the third day. He was trying not to think about what it meant.

That afternoon he and Lonny had siphoned Mr. Finch's Pajero, which was old and gave up forty litres without a fight, and Dr. Moss's Corolla, which was new and had a valve in the neck of the tank that stopped the hose. Fen had sucked on the hose anyway, because he hadn't known about the valve, and got a mouthful of petrol that had nothing to do with the car. He had spat in the Mosses' drain for ten minutes. He could still taste it. It sat behind his nose now, and every burp brought it up new.

Fifty-eight litres in the cans now. Three nights. Then the next car.

He pressed the button. "I'm here."

"Fen from the Trace." Judge always said the whole thing, like a call sign. "How allyuh going up there?"

"Same."

"How's your doctor?"

"Busy."

"Mm." A pause, with the deck creaking under it and a child laughing somewhere far behind. "I hear allyuh dig today."

Fen's hand closed on the radio.

He hadn't told anyone on the channel. Nobody up here had. The grave was behind number nine, on the slope facing the sea, under a lime tree, eleven kilometres from the dock.

"I have a boy here," Judge said, "who could hear a pin drop on that hill. Don't vex. Is just how it is. Who was it?"

Eleven kilometres, over water. No aerial on the island did that. Fen let it sit, because the alternative was asking.

"An old man. Ninety."

"Sorry to hear." It sounded like he meant it. It sounded like he meant everything, which was the problem. "We lose three. Two old people and a lady with sugar. We put them in the churchyard by the market. Is a lot of digging for little fellas."

"Yeah."

"Cookie feed two hundred and twelve tonight. Fish broth. Nell count every bowl, she writing it in a book. Eleven boats went out this morning and ten came back. One engine dead, we tow it." He said it plainly. "My boy's arm, though. You remember the boy's arm."

"I remember."

"Five days. It have a bump in it now that shouldn't be there. I could see it going wrong, Fen. I don't know what I'm looking at but I could see it going wrong." The voice slowed down, the way it had on the first night, when it found something useful. "Ask your doctor what to do. Or ask her to come and look."

Fen looked out at the dark where the town used to be. A few fires burned down there now, tiny, by the harbour. Cookie's drums.

"I'll ask her," he said.

"Thank you, Fen. Keep the channel on."

Hiss.

He went down the ladder, out the front and across the road to the Vales'. The kitchen light was a candle in a jar. Through the window he could see Juno at the table with the exercise book open in front of her and a pen in her hand, not writing. She was sitting very still with her other hand flat on the page.

Fen knew that stillness. He had sat that way himself, last year, at the worst of it, at a desk in his room with the door locked and a screen on that he wasn't watching.

He stood in the road for a minute, then walked back to his house without knocking. He told himself he would ask her in the morning. It was the first time he had ever told himself that about Juno.

Halfway up the ladder he stopped and looked down at the hill below the bend, at the dark between the Trace and the harbour, and thought about a boy who could hear a pin drop.

---

On the seventh morning he was on the roof at ten to six, checking the aerial, when he saw the boats.

His father's binoculars were the heavy black ones from the boat, with a cracked strap. Fen had been using them every dawn. From the roof he could see straight down the coast road to the harbour, the warehouses, the fish market roof, and the dock with the *Lady Marisol* at the end of it.

The boats were going out. Twelve, in a line, small pirogues and two bigger ones, each with a white wake behind it, spreading. A kid on the end of the dock waved them out one by one with something red. Smoke went up from four oil drums in the market square while tiny figures crossed it with buckets. It looked like a town. It was the only thing on the island that did.

He swung the binoculars out past the boats, to see how far they went.

There was a ship on the horizon.

Grey. Low. Too long and too flat for a tanker, too angular for a cruise ship, with a mast full of shapes on top and something at the front that he didn't want to name yet. It sat exactly on the line between the sea and the sky, and the morning light came up behind it.

He put the binoculars down and opened the laptop. The dongle could pick up AIS, the signal every big ship sent out with its name and speed and where it was going. He had spent whole nights watching cargo ships crawl across the screen toward Panama. He pulled up the map. The triangles were still on it from the last time he'd looked, each one with a time beside it in grey, five days old, the last time anybody had heard from it.

Nothing. The fishing boats showed up, a few of them, the ones with transponders. Out on the horizon, where the grey ship sat, the map was empty.

A ship with no name.

He picked up the handheld and turned it to channel sixteen, the one every boat kept open, the one that had been hiss for seven days.

It wasn't hiss.

"—*sécurité, sécurité, sécurité*. All stations, all stations, all stations. This is a naval vessel enforcing a public health quarantine of this island and its territorial waters. All vessels are to remain in port. No vessel is to approach within twelve nautical miles of this ship. Vessels that fail to comply will be stopped. Remain ashore. Remain indoors where possible. Assistance is being coordinated. This message will repeat."

A pause. A click. A man's voice, flat, with an accent from somewhere a long way from here.

"*Sécurité, sécurité, sécurité*. All stations—"

He listened to it three times. On the third he typed it into the laptop word for word, because his hands needed something to do.

*Will be stopped.* Not *helped*. *Stopped*.

*Assistance is being coordinated.* For who? It didn't say children. It didn't say who was on the island at all. It talked to the boats, as if the boats were the problem.

Below him, somebody screamed.

Bix, up on the Hales' tank stand, pointing at the sea. "*Ship!* There's a *ship*!"

In about a minute the road was full. Kids came out of every house, still in what they'd slept in, the Nettle girl jumping up and down with her brother, Olli on somebody's shoulders, little ones who didn't know what they were cheering for cheering anyway. On the Vales' balcony Sol stood with both hands over her mouth. Somebody started banging a pot.

"Fen?" Lyra, from the bottom of the ladder, with her face turned up. "Fen, is it for us?"

"It's the navy," "It's a navy," he said. "Not ours. We don't have one."

She was gone before he could say anything else, and he heard her inside telling their mother. He hadn't said anything else.

The ladder creaked. It was Juno.

She came up onto the roof in the same T-shirt she'd worn to the grave, two days ago. Her glasses were smudged and she didn't push them up. She stood beside his chair and looked at the ship for a long time without asking for the binoculars.

"Did they say anything?" she said.

He didn't answer at once.

"On the radio. You've been listening. You're always listening." She turned and looked at him. "Did they say anything?"

The handheld was in his lap, and the loop was still running, low, *all stations, all stations*. He put his thumb on the volume knob and turned it down until there was nothing.

"They're not talking to us," he said.

It was true.

"But they've seen us."

"They've seen the island."

Juno looked back at the ship. Her hand at her side opened and closed.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. That's okay." She breathed out. "If they've seen us, they'll send something. Even one boat. One doctor. Bags." Her voice caught on the last word and kept going. "They'd have bags on a ship like that. They'd have everything."

Fen said nothing. He had learned on the first night what it cost him to say the wrong thing on the radio.

She put her hand on his shoulder, briefly, the way she did, and went back down the ladder. A minute later he heard her voice in the road, louder than he had heard it in a week.

"They've seen us. They know we're here. Somebody's coming."

The road cheered. Somebody banged the pot again.

Fen sat on the roof with the handheld in his lap and the volume at zero.

---

The ship didn't move all day.

The kids took turns watching it from the Hales' balcony, two at a time, with the binoculars Fen let them borrow. In the afternoon Ida Moss and the Nettle girl and half the little ones painted a bedsheet with house paint from the Finches' garage and laid it out on the flat white floor of the quarry, weighed down with bricks: *24 KIDS. SEND HELP*. Twenty-four was Juno's number. It was in the book under Day 7, with thirteen and twenty and twenty-two crossed out above it. Fen carried the paint tins and held one corner of the sheet while the Nettle girl did the S. Bix wanted the S and the Nettle girl wouldn't give up the brush, so he kicked the tin, and a leg of the K went grey, and they left it. Nobody asked him what he thought it would do, and he didn't say.

From the roof he could see the balcony. Sometime after lunch Lyra had gone down to the bend and come back with Kit by the wrist, and now the two of them had the binoculars between them, Lyra talking with both hands. At one point Kit laughed, head back, at something he couldn't hear. He watched that for longer than he had watched the ship all day.

At nine the handheld clicked.

"Trace."

"I'm here."

"You hear the ship," Judge said.

It wasn't a question. Fen didn't make it into one.

"Every boat with a radio hear it, Fen. Every fisherman's son on this island know what *will be stopped* mean. It mean what it say." The deck creaked. "I stand up on a fish box in the market this morning and I tell them. Two hundred and thirty now. I tell them straight. Nobody coming. The ship is not for us. The ship is to keep us here."

Fen looked up at the quarry. In the starlight the sheet was a pale square, the letters black on it.

"You tell yours?" Judge said.

The silence went on too long.

"No," Judge said. "I didn't think so. The doctor girl don't know."

"I know it." The voice was gentle. "Because if she knew, she would have come down here already. When nobody coming, you go where the work is. Is what I would do. Is what she would do. I read about her in the paper."

"Good man. Keep the channel on."

Hiss.

Fen put the handheld down on the roof beside him and sat with his elbows on his knees.

The ship, he didn't know. He knew about one more day. At the worst of it, in this house, that had been the whole plan, and it had been enough, more than once, until it wasn't needed.

# Chapter 7 — Heat

Tock Vale had been running a fever for three days, and he hadn't told anybody, because if Juno found out she would take the baby away from him.

He was hot all the time, without any of the shivering that came with being sick. He was hot the way the road was hot at two in the afternoon, from the inside out, and it didn't break. He woke up in a sheet so wet he had to hang it on the balcony rail. He drank water until his stomach sloshed and he was still thirsty. When he held Vee against his chest she went pink and sweaty in five minutes and squirmed off him like he was a radiator, but he told himself it was the weather. It was November. It was thirty-one degrees. Everybody was hot.

On the sixth morning, the day after the funeral, he was in Granny's kitchen boiling water.

That was his job. Nobody had given it to him; he had just started doing it on the second day and kept going. The ward at the Mosses' went through forty litres of water a day, a teaspoon at a time, and every drop of it had to be boiled and cooled first, because Juno said the tanks were fine for washing and not for putting in a sleeper's mouth. Then there were the bottles. Vee, and the Lark baby, who had come up to the bend with her brothers on the first night, and the little one from the road whose name nobody knew yet. Three babies, four bottles each, every day. Every bottle boiled. Every teat boiled. Formula measured with the little scoop, levelled off with a knife, the way the tin said.

He had a system. Big pot on the back burner for the ward water, small pot on the front for the bottles. Cool the ward water in the buckets on the floor. Fill the jugs. Bix runs the jugs down to the Mosses' on Olli's old wagon. Repeat.

Bix was eleven and hadn't left his side in five days. He was in the living room now with Vee on a towel on the floor, singing her something off the radio, badly, which she liked.

The big pot was halfway to boiling when the flame went yellow.

Tock looked at it. The ring of blue under the pot shrank, flickered orange at the edges, and went out with a small pop. The smell of gas hung in the air for a second and was gone.

"No," he said. "No, no, no."

The cylinder under the counter was empty. He knew it before he tipped it; it was too light, it rocked like a drum. There were thirteen more cylinders in the other eight kitchens on the road. Juno had a list of them in the exercise book, one a day, and the next one on the list was the Pells'. He went two doors down and came back with the Pells' cylinder on his shoulder, a blue one, the paint gone to rust at the foot ring where it had stood in water, and got down on the kitchen floor to change it.

The regulator wouldn't seat.

He had watched Granny do this a hundred times. You pushed the regulator down onto the valve and turned the collar until it clicked. He pushed. He turned. It didn't click. He took it off and put it back on and it still didn't click. When the new cylinder hissed at him through the gap, he pulled it off before the kitchen filled with gas.

In the living room, Vee started to cry.

The real cry this time, the thin furious one, building. Bix's singing stopped.

"Tock, she won't stop."

"Pick her up."

"I did, she won't—"

"*Pick her up*, Bix."

He tried the regulator again. His hands were slippery with sweat and the collar slid under his fingers. The big pot sat on the dead burner with the water in it gone still and barely warm, and the jugs at the Mosses' would be empty by ten, and Juno would be standing in that room with the teaspoon and nothing to put in it. Vee screamed. Bix said his name again. The regulator wouldn't click.

He stood up and grabbed the pot with both hands, to get it off the stove, to do something, and the heat went out of him.

It went down his arms the way a cold drink goes down, except backwards and the wrong temperature. It went into his palms and out of them, and the steel of the pot ticked under his hands, sharp, three times. The water inside shivered. Then it rolled. Big lazy bubbles came up from the bottom, then fast ones, then the whole pot was at a boil, spitting at his wrists, steam coming up into his face.

He let go.

The pot rocked on the stove. It went on boiling for a few seconds by itself before it calmed down and sat there, steaming.

The black plastic handles had melted. Both of them. There were dents in them shaped like his fingers.

He looked at his hands. They were red across the palms, like he'd been clapping too hard. They didn't hurt. They didn't hurt at all. He pressed a thumb into the middle of his left palm, hard, and the white mark came and went and that was all.

He put one hand flat on the counter to hold himself up.

When he lifted it, there was a mark.

A handprint, on Granny's wooden counter, in black. Every finger. The lines of the palm, even. Smoke came up off it in a thin thread and the kitchen smelled like the time the toast caught.

In the living room, Vee was still screaming.

"Bix," he said, and his voice came out strange. "Keep her. Keep her in there. Don't bring her in here."

"What's burning?"

"*Keep her in there.*"

He stood in the kitchen with his hands held out in front of him, away from everything, away from his own body, and didn't move.

---

He scrubbed the counter for an hour.

Granny's scouring powder, the green tin under the sink. Granny's brush. Granny's counter, which she had wiped down every single night of his life before she went to bed, in a circle, left to right, and then hung the cloth over the tap to dry. The black came off the top in a grey paste, but under it the wood was burned. The handprint went down into the grain. He scrubbed until his arms ached and it was still there, paler, a ghost of a hand, five fingers and a palm on the place where Granny used to cut bread.

He was twelve the last time he'd put his hand on something in this kitchen in anger. It had been the bathroom door, hollow, cheap, and his fist had gone straight through it after a fight with Juno about nothing he could remember now. Granny had come out of her room in her headtie and looked at the hole and then at him. She had made him kneel on these tiles, right here by the stove.

"That temper is a fire," she'd said, standing over him. "And fire belongs to the devil. You pray it out of you, Tobias. Or one day it will burn this house down with you inside."

He had prayed. He had knelt on the tile till his knees went numb and asked God to take it out. It hadn't gone anywhere, so he had learned to put it somewhere else instead. Kick the leg of the table. Not hard. Then harder. Take it out on things that couldn't feel it.

He hung the cloth over the tap to dry.

For the rest of the day he didn't touch the baby.

He boiled water on the new cylinder once Lonny got the regulator on, in thirty seconds, without even looking. He made the bottles with Granny's oven gloves on, the thick quilted ones with the chillies printed on them. Bix laughed at him until Tock looked at him, and then he stopped. When Vee cried he sent Bix. When Bix couldn't settle her he sent her down the road to Sol, who held her too carefully and talked to her about Arsenal. He could hear her from the kitchen, going on and on. Every time she did, his hands got hot again, so he put them under the cold tap and held them there until the water ran warm.

In the afternoon Sol had to go up to the ward, so Vee went down to the bend instead. She came back an hour later asleep on Bix's shoulder, which nobody had managed since the night Tock found her.

"Kit did a thing," Bix said, impressed. "With her thumb, on the forehead. Like this." He showed it on the air. "And she talked to her about Everton the whole time. Kit says Everton's better for sleeping. Kit says Arsenal's too exciting."

"Kit's never watched Arsenal," Tock said.

"Kit says you look sick and you should drink water."

Tock put his hands back under the tap.

He told everyone it was the fever. He told Juno he didn't want Vee to catch it. Juno, who hadn't slept in a bed since the first night, looked at him for half a second longer than usual and then said, "Fine. Drink water," and went back down to the ward. He had counted on that.

On the seventh morning there was a ship.

The screaming reached him in the kitchen and he ran out onto the balcony. The whole road was out, and Bix was up on the Hales' tank stand pointing at the sea, and there it was, grey, flat, sitting on the edge of the world. Kids were jumping. Somebody was banging a pot. Sol was crying and laughing. Across the road, Fen was on his roof with the radio, not moving. When Juno came out into the road and said *somebody's coming* in a voice he hadn't heard her use since before, his whole chest went loose.

Somebody's coming. Grown people, with a ship. Somebody to take the babies and the sleepers and the counting off Juno. Somebody to look at his hands and tell him what was wrong with them.

He went to tell Granny.

The ward was empty except for the sleepers and the smell. Sour milk. Fourteen mouths fed off a teaspoon for six days, and no one brushing their teeth. Mr. Hale's place by the wall had a clean sheet on it now, folded, and nobody had moved anyone into it.

She breathed. Twelve.

He looked at her hands on the sheet. Small, the knuckles big, a gold ring she had never once taken off.

"You were right, though," he said. His voice went low. "About me. You were right."

He didn't decide to kneel properly. He just found himself doing it, sitting back on his heels, with his hands pressed flat on the tile floor of Dr. Moss's front room, because that was how you did it. You knelt and you put your hands down and you asked. *Take it out of me.* He shut his eyes. *Take it out. I'll do anything. Take it out before I touch her.*

The floor got warm under his palms.

The more he asked, the hotter it got, and it all went down his arms into the floor.

The tile cracked.

A sharp pop, loud in the quiet room, like a stone hitting a windscreen. A line shot out from under his right hand, across the tile and into the next one.

He opened his eyes.

Ida Moss was standing in the doorway with a plastic basin in her arms, looking at him.

He thought she would scream, or run and get Juno. She came in and put the basin down on the dining table, carefully, like it was full, though it was empty. Then she came over and stood looking down at the floor beside Granny's mattress, at the crack, and the two pale patches on the tile where his hands had been, and the faint smell of hot stone.

Ida was sixteen, the same as him. She'd been in his year at school since Form One and he had never liked her. She went to church three times a week and told people when they were cursing. She talked like Granny, every sentence finished properly, like she was reading it off a card.

"No."

He looked up at her. "No."

It's what the body does about the sickness." She looked at his face. "You're not sick, Tock. You've been hot for three days. Everybody's noticed."

"I know what Granny Dot said. I know exactly what she said. She said it about half the road. She said it about my mother once, for wearing red to a funeral." Ida's mouth went tight. "Your grandmother is a good woman, and she was wrong about some things. People can be both. My mother is the same."

Ida looked at the window. On the road outside, they were still cheering.

"You boiled water," she said. "Juno told me the jugs were full this morning before six. Forty litres. For people who can't drink." She turned back to him. "I don't know what it is. I don't think anybody knows what any of this is. But I know the devil doesn't boil water for sleepers. If you burn somebody on purpose, Tock Vale, then you come and find me and we'll talk about sin. He didn't know.

They found out in Granny's kitchen, with the door shut and the cylinder turned off. He put both hands on the sides of the big pot while Ida stood by the sink with her arms folded, and he tried to be angry. He thought about the regulator. He thought about the crib in the Pells' back room on the first night, the one he had looked straight at from the doorway with a torch and walked past. His hands went hot, too hot. The water went from still to boiling in two seconds and spat and burned his wrists. He let go and swore, and Ida said "Language," without looking up.

"Again," she said. "Not angry this time."

"How else?"

"I don't know. You're the one with the hands."

He stood with his palms on the pot. He tried to think of nothing, and that didn't work. Then, because it was the thing he thought about most anyway, he thought about Vee. Asleep on his chest in the armchair on the first night with her fist in his T-shirt. The weight of her. How he hadn't moved for four hours and didn't want to.

The heat went down his arms slowly this time. It came out of his palms like water out of a hose turned halfway. The steel warmed. Small bubbles came up at the edges. Then a simmer. Then a boil, a calm one, the water rolling over and over without spitting.

He held it there for a minute. Then he took his hands away, and the water slowed, and stopped.

His legs went. Ida got a chair under him somehow. He was dizzier than he had been after the fete at fourteen, and his tongue had stuck to the roof of his mouth. He drank three glasses of water and ate a whole tin of sardines out of the tin with his fingers while Ida watched with her arms folded and didn't say anything about manners.

"That's one pot," she said.

"Yeah."

"The ward needs eight a day."

"Yeah," Tock said. "I know."

That evening, after dark, he went down the road to Sol's and held out his arms for the baby.

Sol looked at him, and at his hands, and gave her over without a word. Vee grabbed his T-shirt in one fist and his eyebrow in the other, right on the old scar from the fete, and pulled. His hands were cool against her back. He stood in Sol's doorway with the mosquitoes coming in past him until Sol said to come in or go home.

They were at the Vales' kitchen table at ten that night when Fen came in with the radio.

All of them except Lyra, who was home with their mother. Juno at the head, where Granny used to sit, with the exercise book. Lonny with his chair tipped back against the wall. Sol with her knees up on her chair. Tock at the end, with his hands flat on Granny's table and Vee asleep on a pile of towels in the living room, right next door, where he could hear her breathing through the open door. "Don't touch the table," Lonny said. "That's mahogany. Granny will wake up for that one."

Fen didn't sit down. He put the handheld in the middle of the table, between the candle jar and the exercise book, and looked at the ground beside Juno's foot, the way he did.

"Judge asked for you again," he said.

Juno put her pen down.

"The boy's arm. It's setting wrong. He asked two nights ago too." Fen stopped, and Tock watched him decide whether to say the next part. "I didn't tell you. I should have. He wants you at the fish market tomorrow. He said if you don't come down, he'll send somebody up the hill to ask you nice."

The heat came up in Tock so fast he didn't feel it start. It came from his stomach, from the place where it lived. It came from the word *nice* in the mouth of somebody he had never met who had two hundred kids and a radio and a harbour full of fellas who did what he said, and who wanted his sister, and who thought he could send somebody up this hill and get her. "Let him send," Tock said. "Let him send who he want. They could come up the hill and ask me nice."

He stood up.

He didn't mean to put his hands on the table. They were already there. He stood up and pushed, and the heat went out of him into the wood as it had gone into the pot, all at once, and the table smoked under his palms.

He snatched his hands back.

Two black handprints on Granny's mahogany table, side by side, with thin smoke coming up off them in the candlelight. Burned down into the grain.

Lonny's chair came down. Sol's knees came down off the chair. Fen took one step back from the table, the only step he took all night. In the next room, Vee sighed in her sleep and didn't wake.

Tock stood with his hands held up and out, the way you'd hold them if somebody had a gun on you. He waited for Juno to say it. *What's wrong with you. What did you do. Get away from the baby.* Or the other thing, the thing Ida had said. *It isn't a sin.*

Juno didn't say either one.

She looked at the handprints for a long time. Then she looked past him, at the stove, at the gas cylinder under the counter, one of thirteen on her list. Then at the pot on the back burner, still half full of tomorrow's water.

He watched her do the sum. He had watched her do it his whole life.

"How hot," Juno said, "can you get it?"



# Chapter 8 — Market

Juno went down the hill on the eighth day because a boy had a broken arm and she was the only person on the island who had read how to set one.

That was the whole argument. Fen had made it at the kitchen table the night before, in the fewest words possible, with the handheld radio between them like evidence. Judge had a boy with a broken arm, a girl who had swallowed something, two hundred kids at the fish market and nobody who knew what to do. He had asked, twice, for the Vale girl. He had been polite about it. He had been polite about it in a way that made Tock stand up.

"No," Tock said.

"It's not your call," Juno said.

"It's not yours either. Six of us. That's what you said."

"Six of us. Fine. Hands." She looked round the table. "Who thinks a boy should have a crooked arm for the rest of his life because we're scared of a fish market."

Nobody put a hand up. Lonny said he'd walk her down. Fen said he'd stay on the radio. Tock said nothing, which was worse. In the morning he was at the bend with Vee on his hip, and he didn't say goodbye, he said, "Come back," and when she said, "Obviously," he didn't smile.

Juno took it. "I'll bring it back."

Lonny laughed. Juno looked at Ambrose on his mattress, then at Kit, and for a second it seemed she might say something else. She said, "Okay," and they went.

Nothing on the road moved. Cars sat where they'd stopped, doors open, a bag of groceries on a passenger seat gone soft in the heat. Dogs watched from the yards. At the junction the Harker boys were sitting on the roof of the gas station with their legs hanging down, three of them. They watched Juno and Lonny go past without a word. When the youngest one raised a hand, Lonny raised one back. There was a padlock on the pump. The boy's hand had left a brown mark on the roof where he'd been resting it.
