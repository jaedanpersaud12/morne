# 15. Prose fundamentals: how the pros build it

Four systems cover most of what professional commercial fiction does at the sentence and scene level. Each one maps onto a rule in [14](14-writing-rules.md).

## 1. Rhythm: Gary Provost's "write music"

The most-quoted lesson in sentence craft is Provost's demonstration that a run of same-length sentences drones, and that varying length creates music: a long sentence that builds, then a short one that lands. Two things people miss: the variation has to be real (7, 9, 12, 13 words in a row is still a drone), and sentence *openings* need variety as much as length, since subject-verb-object on repeat flattens prose no matter the word count.

*For us:* this is the fix for both tics we hit on day one. "And, and, and" is one long drone; staccato is one short drone. The target is a paragraph where the lengths look like 23, 6, 15, 31, 4.

Sources: Provost, *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing* (1985); [Purdue Global on sentence variety](https://purdueglobalwriting.center/writing-powerful-sentences-2/); [Novela on prose rhythm](https://novela.so/en/blog/how-to-use-prose-rhythm).

## 2. Scene structure: Dwight Swain's Scene and Sequel

The standard model behind most thrillers. A **Scene** is goal, conflict, disaster: the character wants something, something opposes them, and it ends worse than it started. A **Sequel** is reaction, dilemma, decision: the character feels it, weighs the options, picks one, and that decision is the goal of the next Scene. Below that sits the **Motivation-Reaction Unit**: something happens outside the character (motivation), then the character responds, in this order: feeling, reflex action, then speech or deliberate action. Cause always before effect, and never skip the feeling.

*For us:* "set up, complicate, turn" is a Scene. Our first drafts were missing the Sequel: Imani never got a beat to feel the gate kids before the next Scene started. Each POV character gets one short Sequel per chapter, and the chapter's end-turn becomes the next chapter's goal. When something hits a character, write the gut reaction before the dialogue.

Sources: Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965); [Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel); [Ingermanson on Scene/Sequel](https://advancedfictionwriting.com/?p=349).

## 3. Invisibility: Elmore Leonard's rules

Leonard's ten rules all serve one idea: stay invisible so the story shows instead of tells. The ones that bite: never open with weather unless it's a character reacting to it; use "said" and nothing else; never an adverb on "said"; don't describe places and things in detail; skip the parts readers skip; and the line everyone remembers, "if it sounds like writing, rewrite it." Stephen King's version is the same idea aimed at adverbs.

*For us:* Chapter 1 opens with dust, which is weather, but Nia's father is reacting to it in the first lines, which is the exception Leonard allows. Every simile pass is "if it sounds like writing, rewrite it."

Sources: Leonard, "Easy on the Adverbs, Exclamation Points and Especially Hooptedoodle," *NYT*, 2001 ([summary](https://fs.blog/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/)).

## 4. Deep point of view: cut the filter

In close third, the reader is inside the character's head, so words that report perception (saw, heard, felt, noticed, realised, thought, wondered) are a filter between reader and experience. "She saw the corbeaux on the gate" is weaker than "The corbeaux were on the gate." The same logic kills explaining: in deep POV the narrator never steps forward to tell the reader what a moment means, because the narrator isn't there.

*For us:* this is why "standing there was how babies died" had to go. It was the author talking. And it is the rule for backstory: Kai can think about dance only when something in the room makes him think about it.

Source: [a checklist for deep POV](https://dive-into-worldbuilding.blogspot.com/2011/11/a-checklist-for-deep-pov-in-1st-or-3rd.html).

## 5. What *Gone* actually does

Grant drops the reader straight into the event with about three pages of exposition, keeps scenes short, and switches POV several times inside a chapter without ever losing whose eyes we are in. The first book runs about twelve POVs. By the second, readers complained that nineteen POVs made the pace sag and some characters' chapters felt like filler.

*For us:* three POVs carry the book, with outsiders in short interludes. That is deliberately fewer than *Gone*, because the complaint about *Gone* was too many. Short scenes inside a 3,000-word chapter, not one long scene, is the Grant rhythm.

Sources: [LibraryThing reader review](https://www.librarything.com/work/book/200238965); [LibraryThing on *Hunger*](https://www.librarything.com/work/book/88680266).

## The checklist for every chapter

- [ ] Sentence lengths in each paragraph actually vary (long builds, short lands)
- [ ] Each scene has a goal, something in the way, and ends worse
- [ ] The POV character gets one Sequel beat: feel it, weigh it, decide
- [ ] Reactions come in order: gut, reflex, then words
- [ ] No filter words (saw, heard, felt, noticed, realised) unless the noticing is the point
- [ ] No narrator explaining what a moment means
- [ ] No backstory paragraph that stops the scene; a trigger in the room, two lines, move on
- [ ] "Said" only, no adverbs on it
- [ ] The end-turn lands on information the reader already has
- [ ] Somebody paid for the fuel, the food, the water, the power
- [ ] If it sounds like writing, rewrite it
