# 19. Imagery systems and running threads

Added 2026-11-02 after a blind read of chapters 1 to 8 (no bible, no notes). These are the systems the prose is already running, written down so they stay deliberate. Decisions marked **PROPOSED** are Claude's suggestions for Jaedan to accept, change or strike; everything else is a description of what the chapters already do.

## The thesis, on the page

The Sleep took the **permission** out of the world, not the knowledge. Every POV character is doing an adult's job with a child's authority: Juno is a doctor who read a book, Fen a radio operator with a receive-only dongle, Tock a father to a baby he is not related to, Judge a sheriff with stolen shotguns, Lonny a gravedigger with the dead man's surname. Granny's "Now you have to keep it" and Judge's "You're what we got" are the same sentence from opposite ends of the island.

Rule: when a chapter stalls, ask which adult job the POV character is holding without permission in that scene, and what it would cost to put it down.

## Three imagery systems (keep all three alive)

| System | What it is | Where it runs now | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Birds** | Corbeaux on the light pole, then on the taxi roof, "the birds were never wrong about that." The island's own count of the dead. | Ch 1 (the big image), one line in ch 4. Absent at Mr. Hale's death. | The corbeaux come down before every death and nobody says so. They are the book's one honest instrument. Never let a death happen without them; never explain them. |
| **Light** | One lit house on a black island. The drone's unblinking red. The ship with the dawn behind it. Cookie's drums by the harbour. | Ch 3, 6, 8. | Light is *being seen*, and being seen is the danger. The Trace's light is Fen's guilt. The Harbour's fires are Judge's claim. The ship's light is the world watching and not landing. |
| **Counting** | Breaths, bags, tins, cylinders, days, names on a list. Juno's exercise book. Nell's clipboard. | Every chapter. | The arithmetic is the horror. A number appears, then a smaller one. Never resolve a count by magic; a count only changes because someone paid. |

Birds answer the question *who dies*. Light answers *who sees*. Counting answers *how long*. Those are the book's three clocks.

## Granny Dot: the present absence

The most vivid character in the first eight chapters is unconscious. She is alive in three heads three different ways: Juno's standard ("Now you have to keep it"), Tock's judge ("fire belongs to the devil"), Olli's bedtime (too rude to die). Headtie by day of the week. The circle-wipe of the counter, left to right, cloth over the tap. The gold ring never taken off.

Rule: once a chapter, a sleeper briefly becomes a person again (Granny's cough that sounds like any morning; Mrs. Quill's hands opening; Mr. Hale's first name off an electricity bill). One beat, no explanation, then back to the rows. This is how the reader keeps caring about furniture.

The question the book is circling with her: when she wakes, if she wakes, which grandchild will she still recognise? (Rooted Granny, ch 29, is the answer the outline already gives. Keep the question audible before then.)

## Religion and Sunday: one thread, not six touches

What the chapters already have: it happened on a Sunday, during or after church. Granny's white headtie. "Fire belongs to the devil." Tock's prayer cracks the tile. Ida prays over the ward; the devil doesn't boil water for sleepers. Judge buries his dead in the churchyard. The standpipe at the church still runs and nobody knows why.

**PROPOSED decisions:**
1. **Sunday is a clue, not a cause.** The bloom came on a Sunday because the network's cycle is the island's cycle (dust season out of season; see [05](05-network-and-gardener.md)). The kids will *try* to read meaning into Sunday and the book will not confirm it. Ida is the one who says it out loud (ch 9 to 11); Juno is the one who refuses it; neither is proved right in book one.
2. **The church standpipe runs because of the Hills' roots**, not God. Moth knows. The Harbour never learns. Nell writes it in the ledger as a mystery and keeps the column.
3. **The book's real question about faith** is what kids do with an inherited religion when the inheritors are asleep. Ida keeps it and uses it (sin is what you do on purpose). Tock loses it in ch 7 and finds something else in the pot. Judge uses the churchyard because it's the only consecrated ground with soft soil. Granny's version, when she wakes, is the one nobody has kept.

## Romance: timing

The relationships file already maps Fen/Kit and Sol/Lux. On the page, eight chapters in, nobody has looked at anybody. Rule: by chapter 10 one glance has cost somebody a beat of attention they couldn't afford. Fen and Kit at the bend is the natural first (the taxi is right there). Juno and Judge on the dock is negotiation and should stay negotiation for a long time.

## Humour distribution

Chapters with Juno or Sol in the room are funny (the goat, eating *at* the formula, God doesn't want her, the red cricket ball). Chapters with Fen or Tock alone have almost none. That is true to the people, and it means the back half of each movement will read heavier than the front without the reader knowing why. Rule: one dry line per Fen chapter and one per Tock chapter, from the character, not from the narrator. Fen's humour is deadpan about machines. Tock's is about Lonny.

## Granny's kitchen table

Two black handprints burned into the mahogany (ch 7). Keep them. They are where the six sit for every decision from now on, and nobody sands them out. When Granny is carried to the garden (ch 29), the table is the last thing Juno looks at before she lifts.
